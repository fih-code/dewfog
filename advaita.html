if advaita were true

brahman · lens · test
Advaita Vedanta, the non-dualist school the eighth-century philosopher Shankara systematized, holds that Brahman — awareness itself — is the one fully real thing. Not that the world is an illusion: Shankara called it neither simply real nor simply unreal, dependent on Brahman the way a wave depends on water. Look through that lens and much of this site falls into place at once. The hard problem disappears, because consciousness never had to arise from matter. Self-inquiry's missing observer becomes the witness the tradition describes. Near-death reports fit a brain that filters awareness rather than making it. But fitting everything isn't the same as explaining it. Every one of these signals also has a serious physicalist reading — the filter idea, as the antenna page notes, makes no predictions materialism doesn't — and Advaita would sit just as comfortably with the opposite results: whichever way the contested split-brain debate over divided consciousness ends, it has an answer ready. Nor does the lens pick out Advaita in particular; Buddhism, working from the same kind of contemplative evidence, explicitly denies the unified Self Advaita affirms. The sharper question is where the lenses would actually come apart. Perception during an out-of-body state, checked afterward against hidden targets, is one such place — and the one serious attempt came back empty, though under conditions that made it very hard to run. Making sense of everything isn't yet evidence for anything.
